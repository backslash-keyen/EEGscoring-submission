# Task 1 write-up (draft for Keyen to edit)
Numbers come from `outputs/A3_RESULTS.md`, `outputs/partb/B2_RESULTS.md` and `PREDICTIONS.md`. Chance thresholds: exact one-sided binomial at 5% (D15). A3 pooled: 1793 trials, 52.0%. B2 test set: 20 subjects, 913 trials, 52.8%.

## (a) If a model scores 70% on these subjects, how much of that needs motor imagery?
Almost none. The cue is a target on the left or right of the screen, and the subject looks at it. A decoder restricted to frontal electrodes in the first 0.5 s after the cue (the saccade window, before ERD develops) reaches 71.9% across subjects and is above threshold in 25 of 40 subjects. Its pre-cue twin is at chance (48.2%), so the information arrives with the cue and is not a filter artefact. In contrast, the sensorimotor strip (mu and beta, 0.5-4 s) reaches 54.6% pooled and 8/40 subjects, although A2 finds lateralised ERD in 14/40. The motor signal is real but inconsistent across subjects, while the eye signal has the same sign in everyone.

The networks follow this. EEGNet reaches 76.9% and the time-patch transformer 71.6% on all 64 channels. With the eye route removed (local Laplacian, then the 21 Fc/C/Cp electrodes; D25) both fall to chance (50.5%, 49.1%). The problem is not that they cannot fit motor imagery, it is that what they fit does not transfer: training loss falls while validation-subject loss rises from the first epoch (D27). Attribution agrees: per electrode, both rely most on F7, F8, Ft7, Ft8 and Af8 (horizontal EOG positions), and the transformer peaks at 0.2-0.4 s.

A second route sits in the protocol. Consecutive labels alternate (424 repeats against 780 expected, z = -17.6), and the previous label alone predicts the current one at 72.6% with no EEG. A single-trial network cannot use it directly (the previous label is not readable from the EEG features, 46-51%), but any evaluation that leaks neighbouring-trial context inherits it.

So 70% is fully explainable without motor imagery: the eye route alone gives 72%, the motor route under 55%. The exact size depends on the pre-filter (a causal Butterworth gives 65.3% for the frontal decoder, since part of the eye signal is below 1 Hz), but the conclusion survives both filters. A motor-imagery claim on this dataset should report accuracy after the eye route is removed, and here that number is chance.

## (b) Mechanisms behind the B2 results
**Scaling (predicted crossover: wrong).** EEGNet leads at every size (72.1% at 5 subjects, 76.9% at 30) and the transformer closes the gap from ~17 to ~5 points without crossing. EEGNet hard-codes the problem (a shared temporal filter bank, one spatial filter per band, pooled power), so ~2900 weights in that fixed form pin down the frontal eye pattern from five subjects. The transformer shares the front end but must learn how 20 time segments combine, and that freedom costs data. A crossover needs a signal richer than EEGNet's form can express, and a spatially broad eye field is not one.

**Tokenisation (predicted time > chan+id = chan-noid: partly wrong).** Time-patch (71.6%) and channel tokens with identity (70.2%) are within seed spread, but dropping the identity embedding costs ~13 points (57.5%). Without identity, attention plus a CLS read-out is permutation-invariant over electrodes, so it cannot represent where a signal is: ERD under C3 and under C4 are the same input set. Tested directly, its predictions are 100% unchanged under electrode shuffling and left/right mirroring. This matters because the label is itself a mirror variable (contralateral ERD and saccade direction both flip with side), so its 57.5% can only come from mirror-asymmetric parts of the recording, such as an off-centre reference.

**Displacement (predicted time-patch drops most: right in ranking).** A 10 mm slide (a quarter of the electrode spacing) costs every model under one point, 20 mm at most 1.5 points. EEGNet's spatial filters are sensitive in isolation (||W(M-I)||/||W|| = 0.41 at 10 mm), but the projected signal is smooth over 10 mm. Layer by layer, EEGNet's perturbation grows in the depthwise spatial layer (13% to 17%) and temporal pooling averages it back to 13% at the logits, while the time-patch transformer's grows at every stage to 17%, because its spatial filters feed every token and the softmax amplifies a small coherent change.

**Faithfulness (predicted attention > gradient > random: right).** Deleting the most-attended half of the tokens drops accuracy from 71.4% to 57.7%, against 64.1% for gradient x input and 65.6% for random. Gradient x input is a first-order estimate at the intact input, but deleting a token renormalises the softmax, which last-layer CLS attention captures directly.

**Link to Part A (predicted LI-driven, small drop: half right).** Per test subject, EEGNet accuracy correlates with the A2 lateralisation index (Spearman 0.72) and the A3 frontal decoder (0.53), which are unrelated to each other (-0.02). The network uses both routes, in different subjects. However, removing the eye route drops it to chance, not by a few points. This is the largest contradiction, and its cause is transfer, not presence: the motor pattern is subject-specific in strength and topography, and the eye pattern is the only one shared.

**Intervention (predicted no effect: right).** Training with random cap slides (0-15 mm) adds, to first order, the penalty (sigma^2/2) E[(grad_x L . J x)^2], the loss change along a slide. It is only large if the loss depends on exact electrode positions, which the displacement experiment rules out. Accuracy (77.7% vs 76.9%), the displacement curve and filter sensitivity (0.40 vs 0.41) are unchanged.

In short, a cross-subject network on this dataset learns motor imagery only if the shared non-motor route is removed and the motor pattern is consistent enough across subjects to transfer, and on these 40 subjects the second condition fails.
